# Basic Economics You Need to Understand Before Turning 18 — Narration
(Written by Claude from "Basic Economics.md". About 725 words, roughly 5 min at 145 wpm. Voice: George.)

## Hook (30s)
At eighteen, the world starts handing you bills, contracts, and choices. But nobody hands you the rulebook. A few simple ideas from economics explain almost everything: why your phone costs what it does, why some shops boom while others close, and why good plans fail. You don't need a degree. You need four ideas. Learn them now, and you'll see the hidden wires behind every decision. Let's go.

## 1. People Respond to Rewards, Not Intentions (1 min)
Number one. People respond to rewards, not intentions.

A city has too many rats. So the mayor makes a rule: bring in a rat tail, get one dollar. Great intention. But soon, people start breeding rats, just to collect the dollars. The city ends up with more rats than before.

The mayor wanted fewer rats. But what he actually rewarded was rat tails.

This happens everywhere. A school rewards students for hours studied, so students sit with open books and learn nothing. A company pays programmers per line of code, and the code gets longer, not better.

People aren't evil. They just follow the reward. Even you. If you reward yourself with your phone after five minutes of work, guess what you get? Five-minute work sessions.

So here's the takeaway. Don't ask what you want people to do. Ask what you are actually rewarding them to do.

## 2. Every Choice Has a Cost (1 min)
Number two. Every choice has a cost.

Sam has fifty dollars and a free Saturday. Sam can buy new sneakers. Or Sam can spend that fifty on a course that teaches video editing.

Here's what most people miss. The price of the sneakers isn't just fifty dollars. It's also the skill Sam didn't learn. That hidden price is called opportunity cost.

Time works the same way. Three hours of scrolling isn't free. It costs three hours of gym, sleep, or building something.

Every yes is a no to something else. You can't escape trade-offs. You can only choose them on purpose.

So before you spend money, time, or energy, ask one question. What am I giving up to get this? If the answer still feels worth it, great. Buy the sneakers. Just buy them knowing the real price.

## 3. Prices Are Messages (1 min)
Number three. Prices are messages.

Imagine a storm wipes out half the world's coffee farms. Nobody calls a meeting. Nobody emails a billion coffee drinkers. Yet within weeks, coffee gets more expensive.

That higher price is a message. To drinkers, it says: go easy, coffee is scarce right now. To farmers everywhere else, it says: plant more coffee, there's money in it. To inventors, it says: find a cheaper way to grow it.

One number, and millions of strangers adjust, without anyone in charge.

Now imagine a law freezes the coffee price at the old level. The message gets erased. People keep buying like nothing happened, shelves go empty, and farmers have no reason to grow more.

So here's the takeaway. High prices are not the enemy. They're honest signals telling you supply is low, or demand is high. Listen to them.

## 4. Profits and Losses Are Directions (1 min)
Number four. Profits and losses are directions.

Two friends open stalls at the same summer market. Maya sells cold lemonade. Leo sells hot soup.

By evening, Maya has a profit. That profit is a green arrow: people want this, do more of it. So she opens a second stand.

Leo has a loss. That loss is a red arrow. It says: your pots, your vegetables, your whole Saturday could be doing something people value more.

Most people hate losses and hide from them. But a loss is not a failure. It's information. Leo switches to iced tea, and next week, he's in profit too.

The worst move isn't losing. It's ignoring the loss, and pouring in more time and money.

So the takeaway. Losses are just as valuable as profits. They tell you when to cut, and where to move your time, money, and energy next.

## Outro (30s)
So, four ideas. People follow rewards. Every choice has a hidden cost. Prices are messages. And profits and losses point the way.

You'll use these when you pick a job, sign a phone contract, start a side hustle, or decide what to do with a free Saturday.

Most adults never learn them. You just did, before eighteen. Now go use them.
